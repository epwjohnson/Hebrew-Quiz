Rosh Hashanah

רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה

A guide to the New Year service at Westminster Synagogue

This guide explains what happens in the Rosh Hashanah services, in order, and why. It follows our own machzor (prayer book), so every page number here matches the book you'll hold in the synagogue.

Transliterations follow the practice app: modern Israeli pronunciation, with the stressed syllable in capitals, as in sha-NA to-VA. To practise reading the prayers aloud, go back to the practice app.

What is Rosh Hashanah?

רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה rosh ha-sha-NA means "head of the year". It falls on the first day of the month of תִּשְׁרֵי tish-REI, in September or October, and begins the Jewish New Year.

Its names

The Torah never calls it Rosh Hashanah. It calls it a day of sounding the shofar, יוֹם תְּרוּעָה yom te-ru-A (Numbers 29:1), and a day of remembrance proclaimed with the shofar (Leviticus 23:24). That is why our machzor calls it the Day of Memorial, יוֹם הַזִּכָּרוֹן yom ha-zi-ka-RON.

Its themes

  • G_d as King. On this day the world is pictured as standing before its King.
  • Remembrance. G_d remembers every person and every deed, and we ask to be remembered for good and for life.
  • The shofar. The ram's horn is the sound of the day: a call to wake up and take stock.
  • Return. תְּשׁוּבָה te-shu-VA, returning to the right path, runs through every service.

The start of the Days of Awe

Rosh Hashanah opens the ten days that end with Yom Kippur, known as the Days of Awe or the Ten Days of Return. Tradition pictures judgement written on Rosh Hashanah and sealed on Yom Kippur, which is why people greet each other with שָׁנָה טוֹבָה sha-NA to-VA, "a good year".

One day at Westminster

Many communities keep Rosh Hashanah for two days. At Westminster Synagogue, as at some other Progressive synagogues, it has been our custom to celebrate one day only: an evening service, then a morning service with the Torah reading, the shofar and a short additional service.

Your machzor

The High Holy Day prayer book is called a מַחְזוֹר mach-ZOR, from a word meaning "cycle", to set it apart from the סִדּוּר si-DUR ("order") used on Shabbat and weekdays. Ours was published by Westminster Synagogue in 2011.

How the book works

  • Hebrew and English face each other, with matching page numbers. The English translation keeps much of the older "thee" and "thou" style.
  • Instructions in small capitals tell you what happens, for example the ark is opened, in silence or on sabbath.
  • A musical note in the margin marks passages that are usually sung. An open note marks singing by the cantor, and both together mean the cantor and congregation share it. A § marks where singing turns back to reading.
  • A "T" and a page number in the margin, such as t 103, point to a transliteration at the back of the book.
  • A small mark above a kamatz shows that it's a short o (kamatz katan), as in כָּל kol.

          Key moments

          Avinu Malkeinu אָבִינוּ מַלְכֵּנוּ

          Morning service · pp. 41–42 · Ark open ·

          What happens

          The Ark is opened and the congregation sings a litany of requests, each beginning "Our Father, our King". Our machzor keeps 20 of them, from "we have sinned before thee" to "dismiss us not empty handed from thy presence".

          אָבִינוּ מַלְכֵּנוּ חָטָאנוּ לְפָנֶיךָ
          a-VI-nu mal-KE-nu cha-TA-nu le-fa-NE-cha
          "Our Father our King, we have sinned before thee."

          What it means

          The two names pull in different directions. A father is close and forgiving; a king judges. Rosh Hashanah holds both together. The prayer is traditionally linked to Rabbi Akiva: the Talmud tells of him praying for rain in a time of drought with the words of its first and last verses. It is said throughout the Ten Days of Return.

          Listen for

          • Our setting is by the Anglo-Jewish composer Edward Hart. Verses for a solo singer in a minor key alternate with choruses in a major key, which the congregation joins.
          • The last verse changes mood completely, to a lilting melody:
          אָבִינוּ מַלְכֵּנוּ חָנֵּנוּ וַעֲנֵנוּ כִּי אֵין בָּנוּ מַעֲשִׂים
          עֲשֵׂה עִמָּנוּ צְדָקָה וָחֶסֶד וְהוֹשִׁיעֵנוּ
          a-VI-nu mal-KE-nu chon-NE-nu va-a-NE-nu ki ein BA-nu ma-a-SIM
          a-SE i-MA-nu tze-da-KA va-CHE-sed ve-ho-shi-E-nu
          "Our Father, our King, be gracious unto us and answer us. We can claim nothing by virtue of our deeds. Deal mercifully and graciously with us, and deliver us."
          Key moments

          The Binding of Isaac עֲקֵדַת יִצְחָק

          Torah reading · pp. 44–45 · Genesis 22:1–19 ·

          What happens

          The Torah is read from the scroll. G_d tells Abraham to take "thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest" to the land of Moriah and offer him there. Abraham goes. At the last moment an angel calls out to stop him, and Abraham sees "a ram caught in a thicket by his horns", which he offers instead.

          הִנֵּנִי
          hi-NE-ni
          "Here am I": Abraham's answer to G_d, to Isaac, and to the angel.

          What it means

          The Rabbis saw the Akedah (binding) as the last test of Abraham's trust in G_d and a reminder of G_d's covenant with Israel. Its echoes run through the day: the ram caught by its horns is often connected with the ram's horn, the shofar.

          Why this reading?

          Communities that keep two days traditionally read the Akedah on the second day. At Westminster, where we keep one day, the importance of the Akedah makes it the natural choice. It is followed by a short second reading, Deuteronomy 29:9–14 (p. 46): "Ye are standing this day all of you before the Lord your G_d".

          Listen for

          The Torah is chanted, not spoken, following the cantillation marks printed above and below the Hebrew in the machzor. Notice how often the words "they went both of them together" return.

          Key moments

          The sounding of the shofar שׁוֹפָר

          After the Haftarah, before the Torah returns to the Ark · pp. 50–53 ·

          The four sounds

          תְּקִיעָהte-ki-Aone long, unbroken note
          שְׁבָרִיםshe-va-RIMthree broken notes
          תְּרוּעָהte-ru-Anine short, rapid notes
          תְּקִיעָה גְּדוֹלָהte-ki-A ge-do-LAthe "great tekiah": the very last note, held as long as possible

          What happens, in order

          1. The blessing (p. 50), after the verse that names the day: "It shall be for you the day of sounding the shofar".
          2. Shehecheyanu (p. 51), "who hast kept us alive", said this time for the shofar.
          3. First series: tekiah, shevarim-teruah, tekiah · tekiah, shevarim, tekiah · tekiah, teruah, tekiah.
          4. "Happy are the people who know the joyful sound" (Psalm 89:15–18), then three more of tekiah, teruah, tekiah.
          5. "This is the Day of Memorial", with the Torah's words for the day.
          6. "In a cloud of glory" (p. 52): the verses about the shofar at Mount Sinai.
          7. Final series: tekiah, shevarim-teruah, tekiah · tekiah, shevarim, tekiah · tekiah, teruah, and the tekiah gedolah.
          8. "On this day, strengthen us" (p. 53), with the congregation answering "Amen" to each line.
          בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם
          אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו וְצִוָּנוּ לְהַשְׁמִיעַ קוֹל שׁוֹפָר
          ba-RUCH a-TA a-do-NAI e-lo-HEI-nu ME-lech ha-o-LAM
          A-sher kid-SHA-nu be-mitz-vo-TAV ve-tzi-VA-nu le-hash-MI-a kol sho-FAR
          "…who hast sanctified us by thy commandments and commanded us to hear the call of the shofar."

          What it means

          The shofar is the one commandment of the day: the Torah calls Rosh Hashanah a day of sounding it. Our machzor describes it as a call to repentance. The final tekiah gedolah recalls Isaiah's vision of a day when "the great shofar shall be blown".

          "In a cloud of glory" is part of Shofarot, one of three long sections (with Kingship and Remembrance) traditionally said in the Additional service. In ancient times the shofar was blown in the morning service; according to the Jerusalem Talmud it was moved later after the Romans mistook it for a call to revolt.

          Listen for

          • Shehecheyanu is sung to one of the medieval "Missinai" melodies used for the High Holy Days.
          • "This is the Day of Memorial" is set to music adapted by Charles Salaman from Mendelssohn's Elijah.
          • "On this day, strengthen us" is sung to a variant of a lively, major-key melody by the Viennese cantor Salomon Sulzer.
          Key moments

          Unetaneh Tokef וּנְתַנֶּה תֹּקֶף

          Additional service · pp. 56–57 · Ark open ·

          What happens

          The Additional service opens with the Ark open and this poem: "We declare how profound is the holiness of this day". It pictures G_d as judge, opening "the Book of Memory", with every living being passing before G_d "like a flock of sheep" beneath a shepherd's staff.

          בְּרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה יִכָּתֵבוּן וּבְיוֹם צוֹם כִּפּוּר יֵחָתֵמוּן
          be-ROSH ha-sha-NA yi-ka-te-VUN u-ve-YOM tzom ki-PUR ye-cha-te-MUN
          "On Rosh Hashanah it is inscribed, on Yom Kippur it is sealed."

          Then comes the list of who shall live and who shall die, who by fire and who by water, who shall have rest and who shall wander, and finally the turn:

          וּתְשׁוּבָה וּתְפִלָּה וּצְדָקָה מַעֲבִירִין אֶת רֹעַ הַגְּזֵרָה
          u-te-shu-VA u-te-fi-LA u-tze-da-KA ma-a-vi-RIN et RO-a ha-ge-ze-RA
          "Yet repentance, prayer and good deeds can transform the harshness in our destiny."

          What it means

          It is not a prayer of fate. As our machzor's notes put it, although our destiny does not lie entirely in our own hands, our attitude matters: repentance, sincere prayer and acts of kindness can change us, and by changing us change how we face the coming year.

          Its story

          Tradition tells that Rabbi Amnon of Mainz recited it with his dying breath on Rosh Hashanah, after being tortured for refusing to give up his faith, and that he later taught the words in a dream to Rabbi Kalonymous ben Meshullam. In fact the poem is older still: it was known in Israel and Italy centuries before. Many consider it the high point of the New Year service.

          Listen for

          We use Louis Lewandowski's setting for cantor, choir and organ, with spoken passages in between. The opening is a majestic, march-like melody that the congregation can join. The section about the shepherd is quieter and cantorial, and the opening melody returns at the line about repentance, prayer and good deeds.

          Key moments

          Aleinu עָלֵינוּ

          Evening service p. 18 · Additional service p. 58

          What happens

          Near the end of each service, the congregation says or sings "It is our duty to praise the Lord of all". The prayer takes its name from its first word.

          עָלֵינוּ לְשַׁבֵּחַ לַאֲדוֹן הַכֹּל
          a-LEI-nu le-sha-BE-ach la-a-DON ha-KOL
          "It is our duty to praise the Lord of all."

          What it means

          The first paragraph celebrates the oneness of G_d and of all creation. The second looks forward to a day when all humanity will recognise one G_d: "on that day the Lord shall be One, and his name One".

          Its connection to Rosh Hashanah

          Aleinu is attributed by some to Rav, a 3rd-century Babylonian teacher, who is also thought to have composed the prayers that accompany the shofar. Only in the 14th century did it become the closing prayer of every service.

          Kneeling

          Traditionally this was the one prayer at which a Jew would kneel: it was an ancient custom to do so on Yom Kippur at the words "unto thee every knee must bow", in deliberate contrast to refusing to bow before earthly rulers. Because the prayer was heavily censored in the Middle Ages, it came to be seen as a martyr's song.

          כִּי לְךָ תִּכְרַע כָּל בֶּרֶךְ
          ki le-CHA tich-RA kol BE-rech
          "Unto thee every knee must bow."
          Key moments

          The Royal Crown כֶּתֶר מַלְכוּת

          Evening service · pp. 13–15 ·

          What happens

          After the evening Amidah come readings from The Royal Crown, a long poem by Solomon ibn Gabirol, the Spanish Jewish poet and philosopher (c. 1021–c. 1070). They lead into the litany "Our G_d who art in heaven" (pp. 15–17), said with the Ark open.

          נִפְלָאִים מַעֲשֶׂיךָ וְנַפְשִׁי יֹדַעַת מְאֹד
          nif-la-IM ma-a-SE-cha ve-naf-SHI yo-DA-at me-OD
          "Marvellous are thy works, and my soul knows it well." (Psalm 139:14)

          What it means

          The readings move from wonder to humility. First G_d's greatness ("Thou art the one living G_d"), then a string of questions ("Who can proclaim thy mighty acts? Who can declare thy greatness?"), and then the poet's own confession: "O my G_d, I stand before thee in shame and confusion". It is the right mood for the start of the Days of Awe.

          About the poem

          Ibn Gabirol often built his poetry from a patchwork of biblical verses, a kind of creative commentary. Selections from The Royal Crown entered the prayer book, and some Sephardi communities read the whole poem at dawn on Yom Kippur.

          Key moments

          Kiddush with apples and honey קִדּוּשׁ

          Evening p. 20 · Morning p. 61

          What happens

          Both services end with Kiddush, the blessing over wine that marks the festival. The morning Kiddush goes further, with a blessing for each of the traditional foods.

          Wineבּוֹרֵא פְּרִי הַגָּפֶן
          Apple dipped in honeyבּוֹרֵא פְּרִי הָעֵץ
          Cakeבּוֹרֵא מִינֵי מְזוֹנוֹת
          Challahהַמּוֹצִיא לֶחֶם מִן הָאָרֶץ
          בּוֹרֵא פְּרִי הָעֵץ
          bo-RE pri ha-ETZ
          "…who createst the fruit of the tree": the blessing over the apple.

          What it means

          Apple dipped in honey is a wish for a sweet new year. The morning Kiddush also repeats the verse "Sound the shofar on the new moon", recalling the shofar just heard. If challah is used, its blessing would cover the other foods too.

          Listen for

          When Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbat, the evening Kiddush adds the verses about the seventh day (Genesis 2:1–3), sung to a setting by Lewandowski.

          What's different from Shabbat

          If you know the Shabbat service, most of Rosh Hashanah will feel familiar. These are the changes to listen for, in the order you'll meet them.

          The sound of the day

          • Special melodies. Bar'chu, Shehecheyanu, Mi Chamocha and Tik'u are sung to the medieval "Missinai" tunes, kept for the High Holy Days.
          • Shehecheyanu at the start of the evening service (p. 3), for reaching the new year, and again for the shofar in the morning (p. 51).
          • Tik'u (p. 7): before the evening Amidah, the verse "Sound the shofar on the new moon" (Psalm 81:4–5).

          Inside the Amidah

          The Amidah keeps its familiar shape, with lines added for the Days of Awe. In the evening they're on pp. 8–12, and in the morning on pp. 36–40.

          In the first blessing
          זָכְרֵנוּ לְחַיִּים
          zoch-RE-nu le-cha-YIM
          "Remember us unto life, O sovereign G_d, who delightest in life."
          In the second blessing
          מִי כָמוֹךָ אַב הָרַחֲמִים
          mi cha-MO-cha av ha-ra-cha-MIM
          "Who is like unto thee, Father of mercy, who in mercy rememberest thy creatures unto life?"
          At the end of the third blessing
          הַמֶּלֶךְ הַקָּדוֹשׁ
          ha-ME-lech ha-ka-DOSH
          "…the holy King", in place of the usual "the holy G_d": the King theme of the day.
          The blessing for the day
          יוֹם הַזִּכָּרוֹן הַזֶּה יוֹם תְּרוּעָה
          yom ha-zi-ka-RON ha-ZEH yom te-ru-A
          "…this Day of Memorial, the day of sounding the shofar", where Shabbat would name the day of rest.
          Remembrance, with responses
          זָכְרֵנוּ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ בּוֹ לְטוֹבָה
          zoch-RE-nu a-do-NAI e-lo-HEI-nu bo le-to-VA
          "Remember us, O our G_d, for good": the congregation answers Amen to each of its three lines.
          A prayer for G_d's kingship
          מְלוֹךְ עַל כָּל הָעוֹלָם כֻּלּוֹ בִּכְבוֹדֶךָ
          me-LOCH al kol ha-o-LAM ku-LO bich-vo-DE-cha
          "Reign in thy glory over the whole world."
          Before the final blessing
          "May we and all thy people Israel be remembered before thee for life, sustenance, salvation and comfort."

          When Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbat, the Shabbat words are added in brackets.

          After the Amidah

          • Evening: instead of going straight to Aleinu, there are readings from Ibn Gabirol's Royal Crown and the litany "Our G_d who art in heaven", with the Ark open (pp. 13–17).
          • Morning: Avinu Malkeinu, with the Ark open (pp. 41–42).

          The Torah service

          • The readings are the Binding of Isaac, Deuteronomy 29:9–14 and Isaiah 55 (pp. 44–48).
          • The shofar is sounded before the scrolls go back into the Ark (pp. 50–53).

          The Additional service

          The Additional service opens with Unetaneh Tokef and a prayer of confession, both with the Ark open (pp. 56–58).

          Kiddush

          The morning Kiddush adds blessings over apple dipped in honey, cake and challah (p. 61).

          What stays the same

          The Shema, Aleinu, Ein Keiloheinu, Adon Olam and Kaddish are much as you know them. In some synagogues the Kaddish repeats the word לְעֵלָּא le-E-la ("above") at the High Holy Days. Our machzor keeps the usual single word.

          Two endings to know. Almost every line uses them.
          The ending נוּ -nu = us, our: אָבִינוּ a-VI-nu "our Father", עָלֵינוּ a-LEI-nu "upon us"
          The ending ךָ -cha = you, your: לְפָנֶיךָ le-fa-NE-cha "before you", עַמֶּךָ a-ME-cha "your people"
          Most of the requests open with a command: זְכוֹר "remember", שְׁלַח "send", חַדֵּשׁ "renew". For the little words joined to the front (וְ "and", לְ "to", בְּ "in"), see Little words in front in the reading guide.

          We have sinned before you חָטָאנוּ לְפָנֶיךָ

          Additional service · pp. 57–58 · Ark open

          The confession that follows Unetaneh Tokef, sung before the Ark is closed. Each stanza calls G_d by a string of titles, and every stanza ends with the same refrain: חָטָאנוּ לְפָנֶיךָ רַחֵם עָלֵינוּ cha-TA-nu le-fa-NE-cha ra-CHEM a-LEI-nu, "we have sinned before you, have mercy on us".

          Read each line in Hebrew first. Tap a word to see how it's pronounced and what it means, or reveal the transliteration for that line. The English is our machzor's translation, which is sometimes freer than the Hebrew.

          An alphabet of titles. After the opening line, the titles of G_d run through the alphabet from א to ת: אֲדוֹן, בּוֹחֵן, גּוֹלֶה, דּוֹבֵר… Our machzor prints the first letter of each in bold. Turn on Alphabet to see them.
