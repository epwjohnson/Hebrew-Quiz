Reading Hebrew — the basics

From your first letter to reading the prayers with confidence

This guide works at whatever level you're at:

  • Part 1 starts from nothing: the letters, the vowels, and your first words.
  • Part 2 is for when you can sound words out but want to read more smoothly.
  • Part 3 covers the details that trip up even fairly confident readers.

Almost every example comes from the prayers in the practice app. The pronunciation taught here is the modern Israeli one used in the app's transliterations. Where the traditional Ashkenazi pronunciation differs noticeably, look for an note.

Transliterations show the stressed syllable in capitals, as in the app: sha-LOM.

Part 1 · Beginners

Before you start

1Hebrew reads right to left. Start at the right-hand end of the line.
2The letters are consonants. There are 22 of them, and a few change shape at the end of a word.
3The vowels are small dots and dashes, mostly under the letters. A vowel is said after the letter it sits under: בָּ is ba, not ab.
4Prayer books print every vowel, so a prayer can be sounded out exactly, even if you don't know the words. Modern Israeli newspapers leave the vowels out; you won't need to worry about that here.
Part 1 · Beginners

The letters

Some letters come in two forms: with a dot inside (dagesh) and without. For three of them the dot changes the sound, so both forms are shown. The small letters marked final are the shapes used at the end of a word.

Same sound, different letter. Modern pronunciation has a few pairs that sound identical, so don't worry about telling them apart when reading aloud: ח/כ (ch), כּ/ק (k), ט/ת (t), ס/שׂ (s), ב/ו (v), and א/ע (silent). They matter for spelling, not for reading.
Part 1 · Beginners

Look-alike letters

Most beginners' mistakes come from these pairs. Look for the small difference described under each.

ב כ
ב Bet has a little foot sticking out at the bottom right. כ Kaf is rounded with no foot.
נ כ
נ Nun is narrow, with a short flat base. כ Kaf is wider and rounder, like a backwards C.
ד ר
ד Dalet has a sharp corner that pokes out. ר Resh is smoothly rounded.
ה ח ת
ה Hey has a gap at the top left. ח Chet is closed. ת Tav has a little foot at the bottom left.
ו ז
ו Vav is a plain straight line. ז Zayin has a cap across the top.
י ו ן
י Yod is small and hangs at the top of the line. ו Vav reaches the bottom of the line. ן final Nun drops below it.
ס ם
ס Samech is round. ם final Mem is square, and only appears at the end of a word.
ג נ
ג Gimel has a little leg kicking out at the bottom left. נ Nun has a flat base.
ע צ ץ
ע Ayin looks like a Y with a tail. צ Tsadi has a flat base. ץ final Tsadi drops straight down below the line.
ך ן ף
All three are finals that drop below the line. ך final Chaf has a flat roof. ן final Nun is a plain line. ף final Fey has a little hook curling in at the top.
ט פ
Easy to mix up in some fonts. ט Tet is open at the top. פ Pey/Fey is open on the left, with a curl inside.
שׁ שׂ
Same letter, different dot: on the right it's sh (Shin), on the left it's s (Sin).

Part 2 · Getting fluent

The shva: silent or said?

The shva ְ is the mark that causes most hesitation. These rules cover most cases in the prayers:

1At the start of a word, say it as a quick e: בְּרֵאשִׁית be-re-SHIT, צְבָאָם tze-va-AM. In quick speech it can almost vanish: שְׁמַע is just SHMA.
2At the end of a word, it's silent: מֶלֶךְ ME-lech. (A final ךְ nearly always carries one. When it carries a kamatz instead, ךָ, it's said cha.)
3In the middle of a word, it usually closes the syllable, so it's silent: יִשְׂרָאֵל yis-ra-EL, מִצְוֹתָיו mitz-vo-TAV.
4Two shvas in a row: the first is silent, the second is said: נַפְשְׁךָ → naf · she · cha.
If you're unsure, the app's transliteration always shows which shvas are said: look for the e.
Part 2 · Getting fluent

The dot that changes the sound

Three letters sound different with and without a dot:

With dotWithout dot
בּbבv
כּkכch
פּpפf

The dot usually appears at the start of a word and after a silent shva, and disappears after a vowel. The useful surprise is that this works across words. When a word ending in a vowel runs straight into the next one, the next word's first letter softens:

  • מִי כָמֹכָה mi cha-MO-cha: מִי ends in a vowel, so the כ softens. Compare the second line of the same verse, מִי כָּמֹכָה mi ka-MO-cha, where the text keeps the dot.
  • כִּי בוֹ שָׁבַת ki vo sha-VAT: not ki bo.
  • לְמַעֲשֵׂה בְרֵאשִׁית le-ma-a-SEH ve-re-SHIT (Kiddush), though on its own the word is בְּרֵאשִׁית be-re-SHIT.
You don't need to work this out yourself: just trust the dot. If the letter has one, say it hard. If not, say it soft. The rule simply explains why the same word can be printed both ways.

In other letters the dot traditionally doubles the sound (אַתָּה was once at-ta). In modern pronunciation you don't hear the difference, so read those letters normally.

Part 2 · Getting fluent

Where the stress falls

Most Hebrew words are stressed on the last syllable: sha-LOM, ba-RUCH, e-lo-HIM, to-RA. The common exceptions, stressed on the second-to-last syllable, are worth recognising:

  • A family of short two-syllable nouns whose second vowel is a segol ֶ, often with another segol before it: מֶלֶךְ ME-lech, אֶרֶץ E-retz, עֶרֶב E-rev, לֶחֶם LE-chem, גָּפֶן GA-fen. Not every segol works this way (עֲלֵיכֶם is a-lei-CHEM), so learn these as you meet them.
  • Words ending in נוּ ("us" or "our"): the -nu is never stressed, so the syllable before it is: אֱלֹהֵינוּ e-lo-HEI-nu, אָבִינוּ a-VI-nu, קִדְּשָׁנוּ kid-SHA-nu.
  • A few others you'll meet, such as כָמֹכָה cha-MO-cha and סֶלָה SE-la.

The capital letters in the app's transliteration always show you where the stress goes.

Part 3 · Confident readers

Special readings

The divine name

יְהוָה is never read as written. Say a-do-NAI. Some prayer books print יְיָ instead; it's read the same way.

Kamatz that says "o"

The kamatz ָ is normally a, but in a short, unstressed, closed syllable it's an o (kamatz katan). The ones you'll meet most:

  • כָּל kol ("all"), very common, and וּבְכָל uv-CHOL.
  • חָכְמָה choch-MA ("wisdom").

Clue: the kamatz is followed by a silent shva or sits on a short word joined to the next. When in doubt, check the transliteration.

Part 3 · Confident readers

Little words in front

Hebrew attaches short words directly to the front of the next word. Spotting them makes long words much less daunting:

PrefixMeansExample
וְandוְכָל ve-CHOL, "and all"
הַtheהָעוֹלָם ha-o-LAM, "the world"
בְּin, withבְּמִצְוֹתָיו be-mitz-vo-TAV, "with His commandments"
לְto, forלְעוֹלָם וָעֶד le-o-LAM VA-ed, "for ever and ever"
כְּlikeכְּאֶרֶז ke-E-rez, "like a cedar"
מִfromמִכָּל mi-KOL, "from all"
שֶׁthat, whoשֶׁעָשָׂה she-a-SA, "who performed" (Chanukah)

They can stack up: וּבְכָל = וּ "and" + בְ "with" + כָל "all". Notice that וְ becomes וּ u before ב, מ, פ and before a shva.

One exception: a לְ at the front of a verb, meaning "to …" (לְהוֹשִׁיעַ le-ho-SHI-a, "to save"), is part of the verb itself. Unlike the other prefixes, it can't be taken off.
This is why the gold vocabulary highlighting in the app can find הַשָּׁמַיִם when the glossary lists שָׁמַיִם: it looks past the prefix.
Part 3 · Confident readers

Endings that catch people out

◆ָיו is said av: מִצְוֹתָיו mitz-vo-TAV. The י is silent.
◆A patach under a final ח or ע is said before the letter ("furtive patach"): לְשַׁבֵּחַ le-sha-BE-ach (Aleinu). With ע, which is silent, you just hear an extra a: לְהוֹשִׁיעַ le-ho-SHI-a.
◆A small dash between words (־, maqaf) joins them so they're read as one unit, with only the second one stressed.
