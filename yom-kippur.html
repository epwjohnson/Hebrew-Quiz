Yom Kippur

יוֹם כִּפּוּר

A guide to the Day of Atonement services at Westminster Synagogue

This guide explains what happens in the Yom Kippur services, in order, and why. It follows our own machzor (prayer book), so every page number here matches the book you'll hold in the synagogue.

Transliterations follow the practice app: modern Israeli pronunciation, with the stressed syllable in capitals, as in yom ki-PUR. To practise reading the prayers aloud, go back to the practice app.

What is Yom Kippur?

יוֹם כִּפּוּר yom ki-PUR, the Day of Atonement, falls on the tenth day of תִּשְׁרֵי tish-REI, ten days after Rosh Hashanah. The Torah calls it יוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים yom ha-ki-pu-RIM (Leviticus 23:27) and commands: "you shall afflict your souls" (Leviticus 16:29). It is the most solemn day of the Jewish year.

Its themes

  • Atonement. כַּפָּרָה ka-pa-RA: being made clean of what we have done wrong, and starting again.
  • Return. תְּשׁוּבָה te-shu-VA, turning back to the right path, which the Days of Awe have been building towards since Rosh Hashanah.
  • Confession. וִדּוּי vi-DUI: naming our failings, together and as individuals. The confessions come back in every service of the day.
  • Mercy. Again and again the services recall the words G_d proclaimed to Moses: "merciful and gracious, long-suffering and abundant in love and truth".

Between people

The Mishnah (Yoma 8:9) teaches that Yom Kippur brings atonement for wrongs between a person and G_d, but for wrongs done to another person, only when we have made peace with them. That is why many people use the days before Yom Kippur to ask forgiveness of those they may have hurt.

The fast

The fast lasts from before sunset until nightfall the next day, about 25 hours, with no food or drink. Tradition adds other customs of the day, such as not wearing leather shoes. Many people wear white, as a sign of purity and because the High Priest wore white garments on this day (Leviticus 16:4). Anyone whose health would be put at risk by fasting should not fast: looking after life comes first.

The shape of the day

At Westminster Synagogue the prayers begin on the evening of Yom Kippur with candle lighting and Kol Nidrei. From the morning, they run right through the day until the end of the fast, in five parts: the Morning, Additional, Afternoon, Memorial and Concluding services. Many passages are repeated through the day. Our machzor says this is not accidental: the repetition helps to build the unique atmosphere of the day.

The greeting

Before and during Yom Kippur people wish each other גְּמַר חֲתִימָה טוֹבָה ge-MAR cha-ti-MA to-VA, "a good final sealing", or simply an easy fast. Tradition pictures judgement written on Rosh Hashanah and sealed on Yom Kippur.

                Key moments

                Kol Nidrei כָּל נִדְרֵי

                Evening service · p. 1 · Ark open ·

                What happens

                As the fast begins, the Ark is opened and the scrolls are taken out and held before the congregation. Kol Nidrei is declared three times. At Westminster the music begins with the first section of Max Bruch's Kol Nidrei, then the text is sung twice in Louis Lewandowski's setting and finally declaimed by the rabbi. The scrolls are put back, and the Ark is left open.

                כָּל נִדְרֵי וֶאֱסָרֵי וַחֲרָמֵי
                kol nid-REI ve-e-sa-REI va-cha-ra-MEI
                Literally, "All vows, binding obligations, things devoted…"

                What it means

                Kol Nidrei is not a prayer but a legal declaration, in Aramaic, releasing us from vows we make to G_d and cannot keep, "from this Yom Kippur to the next". It has nothing to do with promises made to other people: as our English puts it, "All promises made to our neighbours shall stand."

                The Hebrew page prints the traditional Aramaic. The English on the facing page is not a translation of it, but the version by Rabbi Dr Albert Friedlander used at Westminster since the 1980s, "seeking to return to a closer accord with the prayer's ancient roots". The notes at the back of the machzor (p. 223) give a literal translation as well.

                Its story

                • Its origins are obscure. The earliest references come from the Babylonian Geonim in the 8th century, some of whom opposed it.
                • In the 12th century the scholars of France and Germany changed it to look forward, "from this Yom Kippur to the next", but left the verbs in the past tense, a muddle that remains to this day.
                • The tradition of saying it three times, each time a little louder, goes back at least to the 11th century. It also let latecomers hear it, and made sure it began in daylight.
                • The tallit is worn at Kol Nidrei, the only evening service at which it is worn, because the service begins while it is still light.

                Our machzor's introductory notes (pp. xi–xx) tell the full story of the prayer and its music.

                Key moments

                The confessions וִדּוּי

                Every service · first at pp. 16–17 ·

                What happens

                Inside the Amidah of every service, the congregation confesses together. Two confessions come back again and again through the day: the short alphabetical Ashamnu, and Al Cheit, "for the sins which we have committed".

                אָשַׁמְנוּ בָּגַדְנוּ גָּזַלְנוּ
                a-SHAM-nu ba-GAD-nu ga-ZAL-nu
                The first three words of Ashamnu, "we have been careless, dishonest, greedy"

                What it means

                • We, not I. The confessions are in the plural, so that no one is shamed. Each person can think quietly about how far each wrong applies to them.
                • A to Z. Ashamnu runs through the alphabet from א to ת, so that it covers everything, including what we may have thought but never said.
                • Feelings and actions. Our machzor's notes describe Ashamnu as mainly about our inner attitudes, and Al Cheit about our actions.
                • Six for many. Many communities have a much longer Al Cheit, sometimes with a line for every letter of the alphabet. Ours has six lines, which between them cover the same range: under duress or by choice, in secret or in public, unwittingly or deliberately.

                Some people beat their breast gently at each word, others prefer to focus on the words. Our machzor says that "our community has room for both approaches".

                Listen for

                After Al Cheit comes the sung response וְעַל כֻּלָּם ve-AL ku-LAM, "For all of these, O G_d of forgiveness, pardon us, forgive us, and grant us remission." Then the prayer turns from "we" to "I": "O Lord, my G_d… I am here in thy presence."

                Key moments

                The Thirteen Attributes יְהוָה יְהוָה

                Throughout the day · first at p. 22 ·

                What happens

                Again and again in the prayers for forgiveness, the leader recalls how G_d forgave the people after the golden calf, and the congregation sings the words G_d proclaimed to Moses on Mount Sinai (Exodus 34:6–7). They are usually introduced by אֵל מֶלֶךְ יוֹשֵׁב עַל כִּסֵּא רַחֲמִים el ME-lech yo-SHEV al ki-SE ra-cha-MIM, "G_d the King, who sits on the throne of mercy".

                יְהוָה יְהוָה אֵל רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן
                a-do-NAI a-do-NAI el ra-CHUM ve-cha-NUN
                "The Lord, the Lord G_d, merciful and gracious"

                What it means

                The Rabbis counted thirteen qualities of mercy in these words: G_d's name twice (merciful before we sin and after), G_d, merciful, gracious, long-suffering, abundant in love, and in truth, keeping love for thousands of generations, forgiving iniquity, transgression and sin, and cleansing those who repent. A midrash pictures G_d moving on Yom Kippur from the throne of justice to the throne of mercy.

                Our machzor counts the last word, וְנַקֵּה ve-na-KEH, "cleansing", as the thirteenth attribute, and has restored it to the text.

                Listen for

                The words are sung three times over, twice in the evening service and many more times through the day, to two different melodies: one in the Ahavah Rabbah mode, mainly in three-time, and a triumphal one in four-time. The same passage is the morning's first Torah reading, where Moses hears it on the mountain.

                Key moments

                The High Priest's service עֲבוֹדָה

                Additional service · pp. 109–112 ·

                What happens

                In the middle of the Additional service, the prayers tell the story of the service in the Temple on Yom Kippur, as described in Leviticus 16 and the Mishnah (tractate Yoma). The High Priest confesses, first for himself, his household and the priests, and then for all Israel. Each time, when the people in the Temple court heard him pronounce G_d's holy name, "they bent the knee and prostrated themselves", crying:

                בָּרוּךְ שֵׁם כְּבוֹד מַלְכוּתוֹ לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד
                ba-RUCH shem ke-VOD mal-chu-TO le-o-LAM va-ED
                "Blessed be the name of his glorious kingdom for ever and ever."

                What it means

                These are the words said quietly after the first line of the Shema all year round. They are not from the Bible, which is why they are usually said in an undertone. On Yom Kippur they are said aloud.

                The story includes the two goats: one offered to G_d, and one sent into the wilderness carrying the people's sins, which gave us the word "scapegoat". Our machzor's notes explain the different ways of understanding its name, Azazel.

                The rites ended long ago, with the Temple. Our machzor's introduction suggests remembering the Temple as "the place at which heaven met earth", and the perfect atonement that each Yom Kippur still offers.

                Listen for

                The pattern of the High Priest's words (Ana Hashem), the verse "For on this day he shall make an atonement for you" (Ki Vayom) and the response Baruch Shem comes three times, with small changes each time to follow the ancient ritual.

                Key moments

                The readings of the day

                Morning, pp. 93–99 · Afternoon, pp. 150–156 ·

                What happens

                Many synagogues read Leviticus 16 (the Temple service) in the morning and Leviticus 18 in the afternoon. Our readings are different, chosen for what they say about mercy, holiness and choice.

                • Before the reading (p. 90), the Ten Commandments are read in full.
                • Exodus 33:12–34:9 (pp. 93–95). Moses argues with G_d, asks to see G_d's glory, and hears the Thirteen Attributes of mercy that we sing all day.
                • Leviticus 19:1–18 (pp. 95–96), from the very centre of the Torah: "You shall be holy", ending with "you shall love your neighbour as yourself".
                • Isaiah 57:14–58:14 (pp. 97–99), the traditional Haftarah: fasting means nothing without justice.
                • Deuteronomy 30 (pp. 150–151), in the afternoon: "I have set before you life and death… choose life".
                • The whole book of Jonah (pp. 152–156), the afternoon Haftarah.
                הֲלוֹא זֶה צוֹם אֶבְחָרֵהוּ
                ha-LO zeh tzom ev-cha-RE-hu
                "Is not this the fast that I have chosen?" (Isaiah 58:6)

                Why Jonah?

                Jonah runs away rather than warn Nineveh. When at last he does, the whole city repents, and G_d forgives it, to Jonah's anger. The book ends with a question about G_d's compassion for "that great city", and it is left to us to answer. It is a story of repentance, and of a mercy wider than we expect.

                Key moments

                The Memorial service יִזְכּוֹר

                pp. 176–179

                What happens

                In the late afternoon, the congregation remembers those who have died. The service opens with meditations written for our community, then Psalm 103:15–17, the memorial prayer and Kaddish.

                אֱנוֹשׁ כֶּחָצִיר יָמָיו
                e-NOSH ke-cha-TZIR ya-MAV
                "The days of man are like grass" (Psalm 103:15)

                What it means

                The meditations do not hide the pain of loss. One of them, by Professor David Daiches Raphael, begins: "We should think of human life as a gift". The memorial prayer, El Male Rachamim, asks that the souls of our loved ones be "bound up in the bond of life".

                Listen for

                Lewandowski's setting of Enosh, which our machzor calls one of the finest in 19th-century Ashkenazi music, with its long organ introduction. It is sung with the older Ashkenazi accents, as the composer wrote it.

                Key moments

                Ne'ilah: the closing of the gates נְעִילָה

                Concluding service · pp. 180–202 · Ark open ·

                What happens

                As the sun sets, the Ark is opened for the last time and stays open to the end. Ne'ilah opens with the joyful El Nora Alilah, "O G_d, fearsome in deed, grant us pardon at the concluding hour", and the image of the day is now of gates about to close.

                פְּתַח לָנוּ שַׁעַר בְּעֵת נְעִילַת שַׁעַר
                pe-TACH LA-nu SHA-ar be-ET ne-i-LAT SHA-ar
                "Open for us the gate… at this hour of closing the gates"

                The end of the day

                • Avinu Malkeinu is read one last time, with only its final verse sung.
                • Then the final declarations, said and sung by everyone: the Shema once, "Blessed be the name of his glorious kingdom" three times, and יְהוָה הוּא הָאֱלֹהִים a-do-NAI hu ha-e-lo-HIM, "The Lord is G_d", seven times.
                • The Ark is closed, and a single long note on the shofar, תְּקִיעָה גְּדוֹלָה te-ki-A ge-do-LA, marks the end of the fast.
                • Havdalah separates the holy day from the ordinary week, and the service ends with "Elijah the prophet".

                Listen for

                The Ne'ilah melodies are among the oldest and most characterful in Jewish music. No one knows who composed them; ours come from the West London tradition.

                Refresher

                What's different from Rosh Hashanah

                If you know the Rosh Hashanah services, much of Yom Kippur will feel familiar: the special melodies, the additions to the Amidah, Avinu Malkeinu and Our G_d who art in heaven. These are the changes to listen for.

                The shape of the day

                • A fast, and a day spent almost entirely in the synagogue: five services from morning to nightfall, after Kol Nidrei the night before.
                • Kol Nidrei opens the evening, with the scrolls held before the Ark.
                • The tallit is worn in the evening too, and many people wear white.
                • The shofar is sounded only once, a single long note at the very end of the day.

                Inside the Amidah

                The Rosh Hashanah additions are all here: "Remember us unto life", "Who is like unto thee, Father of mercy", "the holy King", and the prayer for G_d's rule over the whole world. Yom Kippur adds its own blessing for the day, and the confessions.

                The blessing for the day
                מְחַל לַעֲוֹנוֹתֵינוּ בְּיוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים הַזֶּה
                me-CHAL la-a-vo-no-TEI-nu be-YOM ha-ki-pu-RIM ha-ZEH
                "Pardon our iniquities on this Day of Atonement" (p. 13). On Rosh Hashanah the day is called the Day of Memorial.
                The verse of the day
                כִּי בַיּוֹם הַזֶּה יְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיכֶם לְטַהֵר אֶתְכֶם
                ki va-YOM ha-ZEH ye-cha-PER a-lei-CHEM le-ta-HER et-CHEM
                "For on this day shall atonement be made for you, to purify you" (Leviticus 16:30), sung to Mombach's setting.
                The end of that blessing
                מֶלֶךְ מוֹחֵל וְסוֹלֵחַ לַעֲוֹנוֹתֵינוּ
                ME-lech mo-CHEL ve-so-LE-ach la-a-vo-no-TEI-nu
                "O King, who pardonest and forgivest our iniquities" (p. 14).
                The confessions
                אָשַׁמְנוּ בָּגַדְנוּ
                a-SHAM-nu ba-GAD-nu
                Ashamnu and Al Cheit are said inside the Amidah of every service, together and aloud.

                When Yom Kippur falls on Shabbat, the Shabbat words are added in brackets.

                New to Yom Kippur

                • Prayers for forgiveness (Selichot), with the Thirteen Attributes of mercy, in every service.
                • The High Priest's service in the Additional service.
                • The Ten Commandments are read before the morning Torah reading, and the afternoon Haftarah is the whole book of Jonah.
                • The Memorial service in the late afternoon.
                • Ne'ilah, the Concluding service, with the Ark open throughout.

                Familiar, but in a new place

                • Avinu Malkeinu comes twice, at the end of the morning service and near the end of Ne'ilah, with the same twenty verses as on Rosh Hashanah.
                • Our G_d who art in heaven comes twice, in the evening and at the end of the Additional service.
                • Hayom, "On this day, strengthen us", ends the Additional service rather than the shofar service.
                • Unetaneh Tokef may be included at the start of the Additional service, read rather than sung.

                As on Rosh Hashanah, our machzor asks G_d to remember us. Many machzorim ask during the Ten Days for us to be "written" in the book of life, and at Ne'ilah to be "sealed" in it; ours keeps the language of remembrance throughout.

                Studying the texts

                These pages take the prayers and readings that belong to Yom Kippur and go through them line by line, in the order you meet them in the services. They are for reading the Hebrew, not only about it.

                How each page works

                • Hebrew first. The transliteration is hidden to begin with, so that you try the Hebrew. Use Show transliteration under a line if you get stuck, or turn it on for the whole page.
                • Tap any word to see how it is said, what it means on its own and, where it helps, its root.
                • Highlights light up the words that share a root or a theme, so you can follow an idea through the text. Many of the day's poems follow the alphabet, and the highlights show that too.

                Our G_d who art in heaven אֱלֹהֵינוּ שֶׁבַּשָּׁמַיִם

                Evening service · pp. 32–34 · Additional service · pp. 140–142 · Ark open (evening)

                A litany of 34 short requests, sung with the Ark open at the end of the evening service, and again near the end of the Additional service. Each begins אֱלֹהֵינוּ שֶׁבַּשָּׁמַיִם e-lo-HEI-nu she-ba-sha-MA-yim, "our G_d who is in heaven". It comes from the Selichot of the Sephardi communities of Aragon, Livorno and Tunis, said in the month before Rosh Hashanah and, our machzor notes, sometimes in the Yom Kippur Additional service.

                Read each line in Hebrew first. Tap a word to see how it's pronounced and what it means, or reveal the transliteration for that line. The English is our machzor's translation.

                An alphabet prayer. After the opening line, the verses follow the order of the alphabet, from ב to ת. The pattern isn't perfect: some letters have two or three verses, two verses fall out of order, and our machzor leaves out א, ו, ל and נ. Turn on Alphabet below to see the letter each verse stands for. Most verses are built from phrases in the Bible.

                Avinu Malkeinu אָבִינוּ מַלְכֵּנוּ

                Morning service · pp. 88–89 · Concluding service · pp. 200–201

                The same twenty requests as on Rosh Hashanah, each opening with the same two words. It is sung at the end of the morning service, and read at Ne'ilah, with only the last verse sung. Read each line in Hebrew first. If you get stuck, tap a word to see how it's pronounced and what it means on its own, or reveal the transliteration for that line. The English under each line is our machzor's translation, which is sometimes freer than the Hebrew.

                Two endings to know. Almost every line uses them.
                The ending נוּ -nu = us, our: אָבִינוּ a-VI-nu "our Father", עָלֵינוּ a-LEI-nu "upon us"
                The ending ךָ -cha = you, your: לְפָנֶיךָ le-fa-NE-cha "before you", עַמֶּךָ a-ME-cha "your people"
                Most of the requests open with a command: זְכוֹר "remember", שְׁלַח "send", חַדֵּשׁ "renew". For the little words joined to the front (וְ "and", לְ "to", בְּ "in"), see Little words in front in the reading guide.
