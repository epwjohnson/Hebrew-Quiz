שְׁמַע
Hear

The Shema is the central declaration of Jewish faith — perhaps the most important prayer in all of Judaism. Its opening verse, from Deuteronomy 6:4, is a statement of absolute theological conviction: that the divine is one, indivisible, and unique. The word Shema means "hear" or "listen" — and the command is addressed to Israel as a whole, not to the individual alone.

Its roots. The verse itself is from the Torah, but its place as a twice-daily prayer is rabbinic, drawn from the commandment in V'ahavta to speak of these words "when you lie down and when you rise up." Jews have recited the Shema at every significant moment — in daily prayer, at life's threshold, and as a final act — for over two millennia.

Why it matters. The Shema is not merely a prayer but an act of witness. In the Torah scroll, the last letter of שְׁמַע (ע, ayin) and the last letter of אֶחָד (ד, dalet) are written larger than the surrounding text. Together they spell עֵד — witness. To say the Shema is to bear witness to the unity of the divine.

The three paragraphs. The full Shema comprises three passages from the Torah: V'ahavta (Deuteronomy 6:5–9) on the love of the divine and its transmission; V'haya im shamoa (Deuteronomy 11:13–21) on consequence and faithfulness; and Vayomer (Numbers 15:37–41) on the tzitzit as a physical reminder of all the commandments.

The response. בָּרוּךְ שֵׁם כְּבוֹד מַלְכוּתוֹ לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד is said in a whisper — tradition holds it was what Jacob's sons said to him as he lay dying, and as it was not spoken aloud by Moses in the Torah, it is whispered rather than said aloud.

Mi chamocha. The passage comes from the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15), sung by Moses and the Israelites after crossing the Red Sea — one of the oldest pieces of Hebrew poetry in the Torah. In the evening service it bridges the Shema and the Amida, a connection the rabbis called geulah — redemption. The link between the Shema (faith) and the Amida (prayer) through the memory of the Exodus is deliberate and theologically significant. The closing blessing — Baruch atah Adonai gaal Yisrael, "who has redeemed Israel" — uses the past tense deliberately: redemption has already happened, and we carry its memory forward.

חֲנֻכָּה
The Festival of Lights

Chanukah commemorates the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem and the miracle of the oil that burned for eight days. Candles are lit at nightfall on each of the eight nights, with blessings recited before lighting.

Three blessings are said, in order:

1. Lehadlik Ner — To kindle the light
Said on all eight nights. The blessing for the commandment of lighting the Chanukah candles.
2. She'asa Nisim — Who performed miracles
Said on all eight nights. A blessing of thanksgiving for the miracles performed for our ancestors in those days at this season.
3. Shehecheyanu — Who has kept us alive
Said on the first night only. A blessing of gratitude for reaching this season, recited whenever we perform a mitzvah for the first time in the year.
עֲמִידָה
The Standing Prayer

The Amida — literally "the standing one" — is the central prayer of every Jewish service. As the name suggests, we pray standing and face Jerusalem. The following is the Amidah we say on Friday nights, in seven sections.

Before beginning, we traditionally recite a verse of preparation — the Petichah. This is not formally part of the Amida itself, but is included below as its own section.

Note: the Kedushah differs between Friday evening and Saturday morning — both versions are included below.

Select a section to begin practising:

✦ Petichah — Opening Verse
יְהוָה שְׂפָתַי תִּפְתָּח וּפִי יַגִּיד תְּהִלָּתֶךָ
(Adonai sefatai tiftach, ufi yagid tehilatecha)
"Lord, open my lips, and my mouth shall declare Your praise."
1. Avot — Ancestors
The opening blessing, invoking the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and of Sarah, Rebecca, Leah and Rachel. It establishes who we are addressing and why we have the right to do so.
2. Gevurot — the Power of the Divine
A meditation on divine strength — not military might, but the power to sustain life, heal the sick, support the fallen, and ultimately to bring resurrection. The repeated phrase mechayeh meitim — "who revives the dead" — runs through it like a heartbeat.
3a. Kedushah — Friday Evening
The sanctification of the Divine. On Friday night the Amida is said silently, so this is a simpler, personal acknowledgement of holiness rather than the full congregational Kedushah of Shabbat morning.
3b. Kedushah — Saturday Morning
The full congregational Kedushah, drawing on the vision of Isaiah: Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh — "Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of Hosts."
4. Kedushat HaYom — The Holiness of the Day
The blessing unique to Friday night, asking the Divine to accept our rest and sanctify our Shabbat. It ends with the blessing that gives the whole service its name: mekadesh haShabbat — "who sanctifies the Sabbath."
5. Avodah — Service
A short blessing asking the Divine to accept our prayers and rest the divine presence on Zion — an echo of the Temple service, reframed as the service of the heart.
6. Hoda'ah — Thanksgiving
Modim anachnu lach — "We give thanks to You." A full accounting of what we are grateful for: our lives, our souls, the miracles that surround us daily, morning, noon and evening.
7. Birkat Shalom — The Blessing of Peace
The Amida closes with Sim shalom — "Grant peace" — asking the Divine to bless us with peace, happiness, kindness and mercy. It ends with Oseh shalom, the prayer for peace that closes many Jewish services.
8. Elohai Netzor — Personal Meditation
A personal meditation that follows the Amida itself — a cooling-down counterpart to the Petichah. We ask the Divine to guard our tongue from evil, open our hearts to Torah, and bring peace to us and to all Israel.
